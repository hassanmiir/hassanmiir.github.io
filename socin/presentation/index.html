Why not just build one big public data lake? Six reasons. Legal: GDPR and sector-specific rules restrict transfers of personal and sensitive data. Public trust: a citizen who shared data with their doctor did not agree to have it copied to a city platform. Sovereignty: each institution remains accountable for its data and needs to keep control. Six instruments define the space we operate in. GDPR, applicable since 2018, sets lawful basis, minimisation and purpose limitation. The Data Governance Act, applicable since 2023, creates mechanisms for the re-use of protected public-sector data. The Data Act, applicable since September 2025, governs fair access to and sharing of data. The AI Act, Regulation 2024/1689, entered into force in 2024 with phased obligations; its Annex III explicitly lists education, employment and access to essential public services and benefits as high-risk areas — exactly the domains of this project. The European Health Data Space, Regulation 2025/327, enables secondary use of health data in secure processing environments, again with phased application. And the Interoperable Europe Act of 2024 requires cross-border interoperability of public digital services. The common direction is clear: create value from data without letting it leave its protective frame. Federated learning fits this direction, but it does not by itself guarantee compliance — which is why the framework matters.

02 · Federated intelligence

Move the model, not the data.

Federated learning sends the algorithm to where the data lives, and shares only what it has learned.

Here is the paradigm shift, in one sentence: move the model, not the data. Watch the animation. The glowing teal object is the model. It travels from site to site; at each site it trains locally, shown by the amber ring, and moves on. The databases never move — each stays locked at its own site. Instead of collecting everyone's data in one place, we send the learning algorithm to each organisation and share only what it has learned. The term federated learning was introduced by McMahan and colleagues at Google in 2016–2017, first deployed for mobile keyboards, and it is now used in healthcare consortia, finance and industrial IoT. Our proposal is to make it a backbone for public services.

02 · Federated intelligence · Side by side

Centralised fails, federated holds

Let me make the difference visible. Same goal, same four institutions, two architectures, running side by side. Watch the four synchronised steps. Step one: on the left, raw records are copied into one central data lake; on the right, records stay inside each institution and only the model moves. Step two: on the left, every transfer needs a legal agreement — the bottleneck that stalls most public-sector data projects; on the right, only model updates travel. Step three: an attack. On the left, one breach exposes every record of every institution. On the right, the attacker reaches the hub and finds nothing to steal, because the hub never holds raw data. Step four: on the left, the institutions have lost control of their data; on the right, every partner keeps control. The exposure bars at the bottom summarise it: all records versus no raw records. This is why federation is not just a technical preference — it is the architecture that matches Europe's legal and ethical expectations.

02 · Federated intelligence

How federated learning works

1

Share the model

The server sends the current global model to every partner.

2

Train locally

Each organisation trains on its own data, behind its own firewall.

3

Send updates

Only model parameters leave the site — never raw records.

4

Aggregate and repeat

Updates are weighted by data size and averaged, round after round.

Source: McMahan et al., AISTATS 2017 (federated averaging, FedAvg)

Mir Hassan · MRU

The animation on the left runs one complete round of federated learning, again and again. Step one: the coordinating server — run by a neutral public body or the consortium — shares the current global model, shown as teal packets. Step two: each partner trains locally; the progress bars fill while the data stays behind its own lock. Step three: only the updated parameters, the amber packets, travel back — never the underlying records. Step four: the server aggregates them with federated averaging, introduced by McMahan and colleagues in 2017: each partner's update is weighted by its share of the data, n-k over n. The global accuracy bar rises round by round; the numbers are illustrative. After enough rounds we have a model that has learned from everyone, while no one has seen anyone else's data.

02 · Federated intelligence

Federation alone is not trustworthy

Inference attacks

Shared updates can still leak information about individual records.

Poisoning

Faulty or malicious partners can corrupt the shared model.

Drift and heterogeneity

Uneven, changing data across partners erodes accuracy over time.

Opacity

Decisions that affect citizens must be explainable and contestable.

Accountability gaps

Who answers for a decision made by a jointly trained model?

Unfair participation

Small partners are drowned out; free-riders gain without contributing.

Sources: Zhu et al., NeurIPS 2019 · Blanchard et al., NeurIPS 2017 · Kairouz et al., 2021

Mir Hassan · MRU

Now the honest part. Federated learning alone is not trustworthy. Six risks. Inference attacks: research has shown that model updates can leak information about the training data, through gradient inversion or membership inference. Poisoning: a faulty or malicious partner can corrupt the shared model. Drift and heterogeneity: a rural clinic and a capital-city hospital have very different data, and both change over time. Opacity: when a model influences a benefits decision or a school intervention, it must be explainable and contestable. Accountability: if five organisations trained the model, who is responsible? And fairness of participation: small municipalities or NGOs can be drowned out by large partners, while some may free-ride. Each of these maps directly onto a pillar of our framework.

Trustworthy Federated Intelligence · SOCIN

Mir Hassan · MRU

Our framework rests on six pillars, each answering one of the risks I just listed. The top row, in teal, are the technical guarantees. Privacy: differential privacy and secure aggregation, so that even the shared updates reveal as little as possible. Robustness: aggregation that resists faulty or malicious partners and adapts to drift. Explainability: every prediction comes with reasons and an honest measure of uncertainty. The bottom row, in amber, are the institutional guarantees. Accountability: a traceable record of who trained what, when. Fairness: small municipalities and minority populations must count. And interoperability: open standards so any public body, not just the well-resourced ones, can join. Trustworthiness is the combination — no single pillar is enough.

Here is how the pillars become a system. I will build it from the bottom up. At the base, the data layer: each organisation's data stays inside its own infrastructure. Above it, the federated layer: orchestration, selection of which partners participate in each round, and model versioning. 03 · The framework · Privacy

Privacy needs layered defences

Differential privacy

Protects: bounds what any one person reveals.

Cost: accuracy versus privacy budget.

Secure aggregation

Protects: server sees only the sum of updates.

Cost: extra communication rounds.

Homomorphic encryption

Protects: computes on encrypted updates.

Cost: high computational overhead.

Trusted execution

Protects: aggregation inside isolated hardware.

Cost: trust in the hardware vendor.

No single technique suffices. The framework combines them according to risk, use case by use case.

Sources: Dwork & Roth, 2014 · Abadi et al., CCS 2016 · Bonawitz et al., CCS 2017

Mir Hassan · MRU

Federated learning keeps raw data local, but model updates can still leak information. So the privacy pillar uses four complementary defences. Differential privacy adds calibrated noise so that the influence of any one person is mathematically bounded; the price is accuracy, controlled by the privacy budget. Secure aggregation, introduced by Bonawitz and colleagues in 2017, uses cryptographic masking so the server learns only the sum of all updates, at the cost of extra communication. Homomorphic encryption allows computation directly on encrypted updates, but is computationally expensive. Trusted execution environments run the aggregation in isolated hardware, which shifts trust to the hardware vendor. The principle: no single technique suffices; we combine them according to the risk of each use case. A rare-disease model needs stronger guarantees than an aggregated mobility forecast.

03 · The framework · Robustness

Robust and drift-aware by design

Robust aggregation

Down-weights anomalous or malicious updates before they reach the global model.

Drift detection

Notices when a partner’s data shifts — new disease patterns, policy changes, seasons.

Conformal uncertainty

Prediction sets with coverage guarantees, so officials know when not to trust the model.

Building on our research

Proven methods, new domain

Drift-aware federated intrusion detection

Adaptive sign-based robust aggregation

Spectral drift fingerprinting for server-side aggregation

Federated calibration with conformal coverage

Sources: Blanchard et al., NeurIPS 2017 · Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023 · our prior work

Mir Hassan · MRU

Public services operate in the real world, where data is messy and conditions change. Three mechanisms make the system robust. Robust aggregation identifies and down-weights updates that look anomalous — whether from a bug, a bad sensor or a deliberate attack. Drift detection notices when a partner's data distribution shifts: a new disease pattern, a policy change, a seasonal effect. And conformal prediction gives calibrated uncertainty with formal coverage guarantees, so a caseworker knows when the model is unsure and a human must decide. On the right: this is not starting from zero. Our group has developed and published these methods in industrial IoT and security settings — drift-aware federated intrusion detection, adaptive sign-based aggregation, spectral drift fingerprinting and federated conformal calibration. The project transfers them to public services.

Trustworthy Federated Intelligence · SOCIN

Mir Hassan · MRU

Two pillars that matter enormously in the public sector. Explainability: we need federated versions of feature attribution, so we can say why the model reached a conclusion even though no one holds all the data; explanations must be in plain language for a caseworker or a citizen, not only for data scientists; and every prediction comes with calibrated uncertainty. Accountability: a tamper-evident audit trail records every training round; model cards and the technical documentation required under the AI Act are generated as part of the pipeline, not written afterwards; and contribution tracking records who trained what and when. Underneath both sits the most important principle: a human in the loop. The system recommends; accountable officials decide, and citizens can contest.

03 · The framework · Agentic orchestration

Agents operate, humans decide

Monitor agent

Watches accuracy, fairness and data drift across partners.

Selection agent

Chooses which partners join each round, fairly and efficiently.

Compliance agent

Drafts audit logs and AI Act documentation as training runs.

Explanation agent

Answers stakeholder questions about the model in plain language.

Trustworthy Federated Intelligence · SOCIN

Mir Hassan · MRU

A federation of dozens of public bodies is hard to run by hand, so the framework adds agentic orchestration: specialised AI agents with narrow, auditable roles. The animation shows a typical cycle. The monitor agent detects drift at one partner and reports to the centre. The selection agent proposes which partners join the next round — giving smaller partners a fair chance, which builds on our work on reinforcement-learning-based client selection. The compliance agent drafts the audit log and AI Act documentation. The explanation agent answers a stakeholder question in plain language. And at the end of every cycle, the decision goes to the centre: human oversight approves. Agents reduce the operational burden; they never replace accountable human decision-makers.

Phase one, co-design and foundations: workshops with stakeholders in partner cities, the legal and ethical framework, and a first prototype of the federated core.
